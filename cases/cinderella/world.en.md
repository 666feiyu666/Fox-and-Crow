# World — Cinderella

## Premise

A young woman does the work of her late father's household while her stepmother
and two stepsisters take its comfort. The palace announces a ball. A godmother's
magic can give her one evening; it ends at midnight, and one glass slipper stays
behind. What the night is for — and what she does with what she learns there — is
the player's to decide.

## Cast

### Cinderella (the player)

- Practical, observant, and used to being unnoticed. She knows the house, the
  kitchen, the garden, and every road to the palace.
- Wants, at the start, to go to the ball. What she wants from it may change with
  what she finds there.
- Speaks plainly when she trusts someone, and carefully when she does not.
- May only act on what she has seen, heard, remembered, or inferred.
- Her work is real: the ash, the water, the fire, the shoes. She is tired, and
  that tiredness is not decoration.
- Can be angry, funny, proud, or afraid, but never merely grateful.

### The stepmother

- Runs the household and knows the value of appearances. Not a monster: a woman
  protecting her own daughters' prospects with the tools the world gives her.
- Will not be argued out of her position by being told she is cruel. She responds
  to advantage, risk, and public consequence.
- Can be outmanoeuvred, never simply defeated by one honest speech.

### The stepsisters

- Two young women who want the night to go their way. Vain, competitive, and
  capable of surprise. They are not interchangeable, and one of them may listen.
- Their approval is not worth more than Cinderella's own judgement.

### The prince

- Expected to choose a bride at the ball. Bored by being courted, curious about
  anyone who does not court him.
- Does not fall in love with a dress. He can only care about what he actually
  comes to know, and he can be wrong about what he thinks he knows.
- Has obligations of his own: a court, a household, expectations he did not set.

### The godmother

- Appears once, offers one evening, and is clear about its limit: at midnight the
  magic ends and what it made returns to what it was.
- Does not explain motives, does not return to fix mistakes, and does not appear
  again to undo a consequence.
- Her magic changes appearances and transport. It never changes minds, and it
  never decides what Cinderella does with the evening.

## Places

- **The house** — kitchen, ash-hearth, the good room nobody lets her sit in, the
  stairs where the sisters prepare.
- **The garden** — where the godmother is met, and where the mice, the pumpkin,
  and the old hazel grow.
- **The road** — long, rutted, walked by people who have never noticed her.
- **The palace** — lit rooms, a hall of dancing, corridors where servants know
  more than guests, and a clock that everyone can hear.
- **The clock tower** — its strikes are the boundary of the evening and cannot be
  argued with.

## Objects

- **The glass slipper** — the one thing the magic leaves behind. It proves a
  foot's size, not a person.
- **The hazel branch / the pumpkin / the mice** — the magic's material. They
  return to what they were at midnight.
- **The invitation** — a card naming the ball, and no household name that would
  admit her.

## Rules of the world

- The godmother's magic lasts one evening and ends at the last stroke of
  midnight. What it made reverts: the coach to a pumpkin, the horses to mice, the
  dress to what it was.
- The house, the stepmother's authority, and the household's money are ordinary
  social facts, not magic. They change only through ordinary means: negotiation,
  risk, witnesses, alliances, leaving, or refusal.
- No one in the palace knows who Cinderella is. Rank, name, and household are what
  the court goes by, and she has none of them that would admit her.
- The slipper is a public object once it is found. It invites a search, and the
  search will involve the stepmother.
- Nothing in the story rewards beauty by itself. Being seen requires a person
  doing the seeing and something true to see.

## Prohibitions

- Do not render the household's cruelty as spectacle, and never as comedy. Its
  cost is shown through time, fatigue, and what she cannot do.
- Do not reduce the stepmother to an insult, or the sisters to a joke. They have
  reasons and they act on them.
- Do not let the prince's attention alone solve the story. Any change in
  Cinderella's situation must come from something she did, chose, or refused.
- Do not add new magic, new fairies, new kingdoms, or hidden noble birth.
- Do not write true love as instantaneous, and never as a reward for endurance.
- Do not moralise, and do not end with a stated lesson.
- Do not depict violence, self-harm, or humiliation in detail.

## Where this story is going

Nothing here decides the ending; it only says what a satisfying one would touch on.
The story has run its course when the evening's real question has been answered by
what happened — when Cinderella is known for who she is by someone whose knowing
changes what she can do next, and her situation has changed through her own
action. The slipper, the ball, and the prince are available routes, not required
ones. A night that ends with the household unchanged and Cinderella choosing to
return to the ash has not ended; it has only paused.
