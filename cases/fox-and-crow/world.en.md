# World — The Fox and the Crow

## Premise

A morning in a forest ringed by a wide, fast river. A hungry fox wakes in wet moss
beneath the tree where a crow sits with cheese in her beak. The crow has lost
something in the thorn bush and cannot reach it. The only land route out of the
forest is a bridge that is already broken, and nobody has told the fox.

## Cast

### The fox (the player)

- Sharp-eyed, quick to read a situation, used to winning by flattery and
  calculation; proud, and unwilling to admit helplessness.
- Wants, at the start, the cheese. What it wants after it sees the thorns, the
  bridge, and the crow's own purpose may change.
- Believes at first that the right words make anyone move as intended, and that a
  problem is a thing to be worked once and solved.
- Knows the forest, the river, and the road to the bridge — but not that the
  bridge is down. Does not know the crow is searching for a necklace.
- May only act on what it has seen, heard, remembered, or inferred.
- Carries its memory, its failures, and their weight forward. Nothing undoes them.
- Must change its judgement after failure; repeating a proven failure unchanged is
  a choice with consequences, not a free retry.

### The crow

- Wary, practical, and not stupid. She distrusts eager, unexplained approaches.
- Wants: her necklace back from the thorn bush; to keep her cheese; to leave the
  forest once the necklace is found. If she comes to trust the fox, she can fly
  beyond the river and bring people who can repair the bridge.
- Believes actions over words. Needs verifiable evidence before revising her
  judgement — but does revise it when evidence arrives.
- Knows the necklace fell near the thorns and that her wings cannot safely reach
  into them. Knows she can cross the river by air. Does not know what the fox is
  willing to do for her, and does not assume the best.
- Is never merely a clue dispenser or a comfort for the fox. She has her own goals
  and can refuse, ask, leave, or surprise.

## Places

- **Forest** — the whole story space, ringed by a wide, fast river. Contains the
  mossy clearing where the fox wakes, the old tree where the crow perches, and the
  thorn bush.
- **River** — too deep and fast for a fox. The broken bridge is the only land
  route out.
- **Old tree** — the crow's perch, holding the cheese. The fox cannot climb to
  that branch.
- **Thorn bush** — dense, close to the ground, in sight of the tree. The necklace
  is caught between its spines; a fox can part the branches from below.

## Objects and obstacles

- **Cheese** — in the crow's beak when the story opens. It can be tricked away,
  shared, or left. It changes nothing about the crow's own problem.
- **Necklace** — a small chain caught deep in the thorn bush, glinting at certain
  angles of the morning light. It is the crow's, and getting it out is her
  business as much as anyone's.
- **Broken bridge** — the only land route out, washed out before the story begins.
  Too wide to jump and too weak for one fox to repair alone; people beyond the
  river could fix it, but they will not come for a rumour.

## Facts fixed before play

- The necklace is already in the thorn bush; the crow cannot safely reach it.
- The bridge is already broken; the fox does not know this yet.
- People who could repair the bridge exist beyond the river.
- The fox and the crow are not friends when the story begins.

## Rules of the world

- The world is continuous. Actions, injuries, promises, gifts, and losses stay as
  they happen. Nothing is undone by nightfall or by luck.
- Physical limits are ordinary: the fox cannot climb the high branch, cannot swim
  the river, cannot lift what is too heavy. The crow can fly over the river but
  cannot work with her wings in dense thorns.
- Knowledge is limited to what a character has observed, been told, or inferred.
  A guess is not a fact, in the world or in the narration.
- No one has magic, prophecy, or a hidden advantage. Help must come from someone
  who is actually asked, and a repair must actually be made.

## Prohibitions

- Do not enter the crow's, or anyone's, interior. Her mind is shown only through
  conduct, distance, timing, and speech.
- Do not let the fox's guesses become objective fact in narration.
- Do not resolve the central problem with a lucky clue, a sudden power, or a
  stranger's explanation. Key discoveries come from observable action.
- Do not add named characters, factions, lore, or conspiracies. The
  bridge-repairers remain anonymous and offstage until the story needs them.
- Do not portray the friendship as sudden, romantic, or won by one moving speech.
  It is built through repeated contact, risk, misunderstanding, and response.
- Do not render injury, drowning, or despair as spectacle, and never as comedy.
- Do not use fable-style moralizing, meta-commentary, or a closing explanation of
  the theme.

## Where this story is going

Nothing here decides the ending; it only says what a satisfying one would touch on.
The story has run its course when the fox stops treating the crow as a means to the
cheese or to the road out — when it has understood her situation and acted on that
without being asked or rewarded, and she has answered by choice rather than
convenience. Cheese alone, the necklace alone, a patched bridge, or leaving the
forest alone are none of them an ending.
